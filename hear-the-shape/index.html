How it works

Everything you hear is solved, not sampled.

  1. Outline → grid. Your loop is laid over a grid about sixty cells across. Every grid point inside the wire becomes an unknown, usually 1,500–4,000 of them.
  2. The operator. A stretched film obeys the wave equation, and its modes are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, held at zero on the wire: −Δφ = λφ. On the grid that is the five-point stencil, a sparse symmetric matrix.
  3. The solve. A banded Cholesky factorisation (the bandwidth is about one grid row), then block Lanczos on the inverse with full re-orthogonalisation. The block size is three, so a symmetric drum's repeated notes are all found, not just one of each pair. A Rayleigh–Ritz step keeps the lowest ~90 modes that converged. It all runs in a Web Worker and usually takes well under a second.
  4. The sound. Each eigenpair becomes a resonator in an AudioWorklet. Striking at point p excites mode k in proportion to φk(p), which is the drum's Green's function, so where you hit matters. Skin rings at f ∝ √λ. Steel and glass reuse the same mode shapes with the plate law f ∝ λ. That last part is an approximation: a real plate's modes differ.
  5. The picture. The film is u(x,t) = Σ ak φk(x) e−t/τk sin(Ωkt), slowed about a hundredfold so you can watch the wave leave the mallet and bounce off the wire. The colour is real thin-film interference, integrated over eight wavelengths.
  6. Dust. Grains jump in proportion to how hard the film moves under them, so they drift to the lines that stay still. These are Chladni figures, but for your shape.

So, can you hear the shape?

Partly. Weyl's law says the count of notes below λ grows like Aλ/4π − P√λ/4π + c. Fitting that to the spectrum recovers the drum's area and perimeter from sound alone. Those are the "heard" numbers in the panel.

But not entirely. Gordon, Webb and Wolpert (1992) built two different drums with identical spectra. To find them here, every one of the 318 shapes that can be made from seven half-squares was enumerated and its spectrum computed. Exactly one pair matched: theirs. On this grid their notes agree to about eleven decimal places. Press Two drums to hear them.
